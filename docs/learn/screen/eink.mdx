# How e-ink works

<OneSentence>
An <Term id="eink" /> pixel is a capsule of pigment that keeps its color without power but takes
about half a second to change, so Inkshelf redraws only what changed, gently, and flashes the
whole panel clean every few pages.
</OneSentence>

Your laptop's screen redraws itself 60 times a second whether anything changed or not. The
InkPad 3's screen does the opposite: it does nothing at all until it is told to change, and then
it takes a visible moment to do it. Every choice Inkshelf makes about drawing comes from that one
fact. This chapter shows what is inside the panel, and the three rules that follow from it.

<Analogy icon="🧲">
Think of a magnetic drawing board for children: a pen pulls dark filings up to the surface, and
the drawing stays there with no battery. Sliding the bar under the board wipes it clean, but
everything blinks. An e-ink panel is that board, with 2.6 million tiny cells and a computer
holding the pen.
</Analogy>

## Tiny capsules of pigment

Each pixel of the panel holds a clear liquid with two kinds of tiny particles: white ones with
one electric charge, black ones with the other. Under each pixel sits an electrode; above the
whole panel, a transparent one. Put a voltage between them, and one color is pulled up to the
glass while the other sinks. What you see is whichever color sits on top.

Two properties follow, and they shape everything else:

- **It is bistable.** Once the particles have moved, they stay, with no power at all. That is why
  an e-reader lasts weeks on a charge, and why the panel keeps showing the last page when the
  app is not drawing.
- **It is slow.** Particles have to swim through a liquid. Changing a pixel takes hundreds of
  milliseconds, not the 16 ms of a computer screen. They swim even slower in the cold: Inkshelf
  asks the driver to adjust to the panel's temperature (`tempUseAmbient` in
  <CodeRef path="internal/device/pocketbook/eink_refresh.go" />).

Grays are particles stopped part of the way. To land a pixel exactly on one of 16 grays, the
panel does not send one long push but a carefully timed sequence of pushes and pulls.

## Waveforms: recipes of voltages

That sequence is a <Term id="waveform" />: a recipe of voltages, frame after frame, that takes
each pixel from its old gray to its new one. The panel's controller has several recipes, and each
trades speed for quality. The firmware numbers them, and Inkshelf names them in its `Waveform`
type:

| Waveform | Number | Grays | What it is for | Time on an InkPad 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| <Term id="gc16">GC16</Term> | 2 | 16 | The cleanest: drives every pixel. As a *full* refresh it flashes black, then white | 600 ms full, 490 ms partial |
| <Term id="gl16">GL16</Term> | 5 | 16 | Drives only the pixels that change; made for text on white, flickers less | 490 ms |
| <Term id="du">DU</Term> | 1 | 2 | Black and white only, for quick feedback | 265 ms |
| A2 | 4 | 2 | Black and white, fastest, leaves the most ghosting | not measured |
| GC4, DU4 | 3, 8 | 4 | Four grays, rarely useful for books | not measured |

The times are the ones the <Term id="emulator" /> uses to imitate the panel
(`fullRefreshTime`, `partialRefreshTime` and `fastRefreshTime` in
<CodeRef path="internal/device/pocketbook/emulated_firmware.go" />), measured on a reader. Half a
second is the number to remember: that is how long every page turn spends waiting for the
panel, whatever the app does.

A refresh also comes in two **modes**. A *partial* update (`updateModePartial`) only touches
the pixels whose value changed. A *full* update (`updateModeFull`) drives every pixel of the
area, which is what makes the screen blink.

## Ghosting, and the flash every few pages

A partial refresh is not perfect. A pixel that went from black to white is almost white, but a
trace of the black stays. Turn a few pages and these traces pile up into a faint mess of old
pages behind the new one: <Term id="ghosting" />. A <Term id="full-refresh" /> drives every
pixel through black and white before landing on the image, which wipes the traces, but the whole
screen blinks.

So Inkshelf mixes the two. A normal page turn is a partial refresh with GL16 (the reader's
`pageWaveform`): no blink, half a second. Every few turns, the turn is a full GC16 refresh
instead, and the ghosts are gone:

- every **5th** turn in a comic (`fullEvery` in <CodeRef path="internal/app/openbook/openbook.go" />),
- every **6th** turn in a text book (`textFullEvery` in <CodeRef path="internal/app/openbook/text.go" />):
  black text on white ghosts less than pictures.

The app also flashes when it comes back to the <Term id="foreground" /> (another app may have
drawn anything meanwhile) and when a screen changes completely, for example when a book opens.

```go title="internal/app/openbook/openbook.go"
// fullRefreshDue reports whether the next page turn refreshes the panel whole.
func fullRefreshDue(app *state.State) bool {
	every := fullEvery
	if app.Reader.Text != nil {
		every = textFullEvery
	}
	return app.Reader.Turns%every == every-1
}
```

`Turns` counts the turns since the book was opened, starting at 0, so turns 5, 10, 15… of a
comic flash.

## Refresh only what changed

A refresh is not only a waveform: it is also a **rectangle**. The app tells the panel which
area to update, and only that area waits half a second. When you open the reader's toolbar,
Inkshelf refreshes only the toolbar's strip at the top, and in dark mode its second strip at the
bottom, each on its own (`refreshAreaLocked` in
<CodeRef path="internal/app/screens/reader/reader.go" />). When a page turns, it refreshes the
page's area and the area of the page before, in case the two pictures have different sizes.

Asking for a refresh does not wait for it to end: the request returns at once, and the panel
works on its own. That raises a question. What if a second refresh comes while the first is
still running? If they touch the same pixels, the new pixels would be changed under a refresh
that is still driving them. If they don't, there is no reason to wait.

That is exactly the rule: Inkshelf waits only when the new rectangle **overlaps** one still
being refreshed.

## How Inkshelf does it

Two functions do the drawing side of this: `pocketbook.Refresh(rect, waveform)` for a partial
refresh, and `pocketbook.FlashRefresh()` for a full GC16 refresh of the whole screen. On the
reader, both end in `flush`, which waits if needed, copies the area from the app's
<Term id="canvas" /> into the <Term id="framebuffer" />, and sends the request to the panel's
driver with an <Term id="ioctl" />:

```go title="internal/device/pocketbook/framebuffer_linux_arm.go"
// waitForOverlappingRefresh waits for the refreshes still running that overlap
// panelRect to end. Refreshes elsewhere go on meanwhile.
func (reader *readerFirmware) waitForOverlappingRefresh(panelRect image.Rectangle) {
	kept := reader.running[:0]
	for _, refresh := range reader.running {
		if !refresh.rect.Overlaps(panelRect) {
			kept = append(kept, refresh)
			continue
		}
		reader.wait(refresh.marker) // an ioctl: "tell me when refresh N is over"
	}
	reader.running = kept
}
```

Each refresh gets a number, its *marker*. Waiting means asking the driver "tell me when refresh
number N is over".

<details>
<summary>Going deeper</summary>

- **The driver speaks i.MX6.** The InkPad 3 has an Allwinner chip, but PocketBook's panel
  driver (`epdc.ko`) offers the ioctls of NXP's i.MX6 e-ink driver on `/dev/fb0`:
  `MXCFB_SEND_UPDATE` (`0x4040462e`) starts a refresh and returns at once,
  `MXCFB_WAIT_FOR_UPDATE_COMPLETE` (`0x4004462f`) waits for a marker. The request is a 64-byte
  `mxcfb_update_data`: top, left, width, height, waveform, mode, marker, temperature
  (`mxcfbUpdate` in `eink_refresh.go`).
- **Rotated rectangles.** The panel is mounted turned a quarter, so the rectangle sent to the
  driver is the app's rectangle rotated into framebuffer coordinates (`framebufferRect`). The
  overlap test is done on those, which gives the same answer.
- **The last few refreshes are remembered.** `flush` keeps the last four: a refresh lasts under
  a second, and the app sends at most two at a time (the toolbar's two strips in dark mode).
  With three refreshes A, B, C where B does not overlap A but C does, C waits for A. Before it
  forgets the oldest of four, `flush` waits for it, so none is ever overlapped while running.
- **What a page turn costs besides the panel.** The copy into the framebuffer, which also
  rotates the pixels, takes about 22 ms a page turn (`copyRotated`). `flush` logs its wait, copy and send times to `applications/inkshelf.log`: that is how
  page turns are timed on the reader.
- **Visible or not.** Before every refresh, `flush` checks that the app is in the foreground and
  that the firmware does not suppress refreshes; otherwise it does nothing.
- **DU and A2 are unused.** Inkshelf only uses GL16 (page turns) and GC16 (flashes, other
  screens, error pages, and dark pages, where GL16 would flash the area white). The fast waveforms are in `Waveform` because the firmware has them.
- **The emulator is pessimistic.** With `-eink`, the emulated panel sleeps for the refresh time
  after *every* refresh, overlapping or not, so it shows the worst case
  (`refreshTime` in `emulated_firmware.go`).
- **Timings drift.** The 490 / 600 / 265 ms above come from the emulator's constants and the
  package documentation. An older comment next to `pageWaveform` in `reader.go` says 435 ms for
  GL16 and 576 ms for GC16, from an earlier measurement. Measure on your own reader with the log
  if the exact number matters.

</details>
