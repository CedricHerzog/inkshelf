# Pages and positions

<OneSentence>Lines drop onto a page under a cursor that moves down, and your place in a book is kept as a chapter and a count of characters, which no change of font size can move.</OneSentence>

The last chapter turned each block into lines. Two problems are left. The lines must be cut
into screens, which is <Term id="pagination">pagination</Term>. And the reader must remember
where you are, although the pages change every time you change the font size.

<Analogy icon="🔖">"Page 112" means nothing if you switch from the paperback to the
large-print edition. "Chapter 4, the 6,000th character" means the same sentence in both. That
is the kind of bookmark Inkshelf keeps.</Analogy>

## Lines drop onto a page

The page of the InkPad 3 is 1404 × 1872 pixels. The app leaves margins of 90 pixels on the
sides, 80 at the top and 100 at the bottom, so the text goes in a box of 1224 × 1692.

`Paginate` places the blocks of a chapter one after the other, from the top of that box, with a
cursor `y`: where the next thing goes. Each line, image or rule placed moves `y` down by its
height. When the next line does not fit under `y`, a new page starts and `y` goes back to the
top. A few rules make the result look like a book rather than a printout:

- **Margins collapse**, as in CSS. Between two blocks, the space is the *larger* of the first
  one's bottom margin and the second one's top margin, not their sum. A block at the top of a
  page gets no top margin: the page's own margin is enough. Books give margins in ems, so they
  grow with the font size.
- **No lone first line.** A paragraph's first two lines stay together. If they do not both fit
  at the bottom of the page, the paragraph starts on the next page, rather than leave its first
  line alone down there (an <Term id="orphan">orphan</Term>).
- **A heading stays with its text.** A block marked `KeepWithNext` moves to the next page unless
  all its lines, the margin after it and the first two lines of the next block fit. Every
  heading is marked so, and so is any block whose CSS says `break-after: avoid`.
- **An empty page takes anything.** What does not fit even on an empty page is put there anyway:
  the next page would be no better. And the keep rules give up when what they would keep
  together is taller than a whole page.

Here is the whole keep rule, as `placeText` checks it before placing a block's lines:

```go title="internal/typesetting/layout/paginate.go · placeText (trimmed)"
needed := 0
for index, line := range lines {
	if index < 2 || block.KeepWithNext {
		needed += line.height // the first two lines, or all of a heading's
	}
}
if block.KeepWithNext && next != nil && next.Kind != document.Image {
	needed += max(em(next.MarginTop), em(block.MarginBottom)) + 2*lineHeight(*next)
}
if needed > layout.roomLeft() && !layout.pageIsEmpty() && needed <= layout.pageHeight {
	layout.newPage(lines[0].Start)
}
```

The opposite case, a paragraph's *last* line alone at the top of the next page (a widow), is
allowed: Inkshelf does not check for it.

## Pictures, floats and tables

A few kinds of blocks need more than lines:

- **An image** is centred. It is as wide as the book asks, in percent of the text's width, or
  else its own size enlarged up to three times (`maxImageScale`), and always fitted in the
  text's width and the page's height. If it does not fit under `y`, it goes to the next page.
- **A float** (a picture with its caption pushed to one side, a sidebar) is laid out apart, in its
  own width, then placed at its side where the next block starts. The lines beside it are laid
  out again, shorter, until the float ends. A float wider than 60% of the text, or that would
  leave less than 9 ems of text beside it, stays in the text instead. A floated letter much larger
  than its paragraph is a drop cap, sitting on one of the paragraph's first baselines.
- **A table** is laid out as a grid. Its rows go down the page in *bands* (rows that no cell
  spans out of): a band that fits is kept whole, a band too tall for a page is cut between the
  lines of its cells, and the header rows are repeated on each page if they take a quarter of it
  at most. A table too wide shrinks its font, down to 60%, then squeezes its columns.

## What a page is

The result of `Paginate` is a list of `Page`s. A page is everything on it, at its place in
pixels, plus the two numbers that tie it to the text:

```go title="internal/typesetting/layout/page.go"
type Page struct {
	Start, End int               // the offset of the first rune shown, and past the last
	Lines      []Line            // words at their x and y
	Images     []PlacedImage     // image blocks, inline images and formulas
	Rules      []image.Rectangle
	Strokes    []Stroke          // underlines and strike-throughs
	Links      []PlacedLink      // the areas you can tap
	Boxes      []PlacedBorderBox // borders and backgrounds
}
```

`Page.Draw` paints it on a white image, from the bottom layer up: the border boxes' backgrounds
and borders first, then the underlines and strike-throughs, the rules in dark gray, the images
and formulas, and the words last, on top of everything. The links are not drawn: the app turns
them into places you can tap.

## Why page numbers make bad bookmarks

Every rune of a chapter has an **offset**: its position in the chapter, counting characters
(Go calls them runes), spaces included. A picture or a rule counts as one. The offsets are
fixed when the chapter is parsed, before any layout, so they never depend on the font size or
the screen. That makes the <Term id="rune-offset">rune offset</Term> the perfect bookmark.

So a reading position is a `shelf.Location`: a chapter, counted from 0 in the book's reading
order, and the offset of the first rune of the page you are on. When you press A+, the app
takes the Location of the page shown, lays the chapter out again at the new size (`Relayout`),
and shows the page that holds that offset: `PageAt` returns the last page that starts at or
before it.

The page breaks above come from a rough model (every character half an em wide), not real
fonts, but `PageAt` is the real rule. Notice the green mark: the page shown usually starts a
little *before* the bookmark. As the app saves the start of the page shown, going back and forth
between sizes can only move your place backwards, by less than a page. It never skips text you
have not read.

## Progress across the book

A Location is perfect for finding your place, but it does not say how far into the book you
are, and the servers want a page number. So a text book also has
<Term id="virtual-page">virtual pages</Term>: slices of `shelf.CharsPerPage` = 1500 runes, the
same at every font size. That is about one real page at the default size (a rough estimate: nothing in the code ties the two).

The book's index knows how many runes each chapter has, so a Location becomes an offset from
the start of the book, then a percentage and a virtual page:

```go title="internal/reading/textbook.go"
func (text *TextBook) VirtualPage(location shelf.Location, pages int) int {
	return min(max(text.global(location)/shelf.CharsPerPage+1, 1), pages)
}

func (text *TextBook) Percent(location shelf.Location) int {
	return text.global(location) * 100 / text.Index.Total
}
```

The book's `Pages` is the total over 1500, rounded up. The virtual page is what the lists show
and what the progress sync sends to the server. On the last real page of the book, the page is
set to the last one and the book is marked as read. In the reader's toolbar, the app shows both worlds: the real page in this chapter, the chapter's page count, and the percentage
(`PageLabel`: "3 / 12 · 41%").

## Download time and read time

Some work is done once, when the book is downloaded; the rest, every time you read.

<Figure
  title="What happens once, and what happens every time"
  tryThis="Step through the reflowable book: no page is laid out at download time. Then switch to fixed layout: every page is drawn at download time, and reading does no layout at all.">
  <PagesTimeline />
</Figure>

At download time, `epub.BuildIndex` reads every chapter once, only to count its runes and to
turn the table of contents into Locations. That is `index.json`. It carries a version
(`epub.IndexVersion`, 7 today): if a new version of Inkshelf counts runes differently, the index
is built again when the book opens, as old offsets would point at the wrong text.

At read time, nothing is laid out until it is needed: only the chapter you are in, at your font
size. The pages of a chapter are kept by the book's renderer, and the layouts of all but your
chapter and its two neighbours are dropped as you read. A fixed-layout EPUB (a comic, a picture
book) is different: its pages cannot reflow, so they are all drawn at download time and the
book becomes a book of page images, like a comic.

Laying out a chapter can take a while, and two threads want it: the app, which shows the page,
and a background <Term id="goroutine">goroutine</Term> that draws the next page ahead. So the
renderer has a <Term id="mutex">mutex</Term> of its own. The order is fixed: the app state's
mutex first, then the renderer's, never the other way round ([Threads and locks](../app/locks.mdx)
explains why). The background goroutine lays out and draws while holding only the renderer's, so the app stays free to answer your taps meanwhile.

## How Inkshelf does it

- `layout.Paginate(doc, settings, faces, sizer)` lays out one chapter into `[]Page`. A
  `paginator` keeps the cursor `y` and the last bottom margin (`marginAfter`); `startBlock`
  collapses margins, `placeText` keeps lines together, `newPage` starts a page.
- `reading.TextBook` is the open book: `GoTo`, `Next`, `Previous`, `Location`, `Relayout`,
  `Percent`, `VirtualPage`. Its `renderer` lays out chapters on demand and caches them.
- `shelf.Location{Chapter, Offset}` is saved in `book.json`; `shelf.CharsPerPage` is 1500.
- At download, `downloadReflowable` builds the index and counts the virtual pages;
  `downloadFixedLayout` draws every page.

<details>
<summary>Going deeper</summary>

- **Page.End.** A page's End is the next page's Start (the chapter's length for the last page).
  It goes further only when a table row cut across pages shows text from beyond the next page's
  start in its later cells.
- **Turning back across chapters.** `Previous` on the first page of a chapter has to lay out the
  previous chapter to know which page is its last. `Next` only needs the next chapter's first
  page, which is page 0.
- **Empty chapters are skipped.** A chapter with no runes (`Chars == 0`) is never shown: `Next`
  and `Previous` step over it, and a book with no saved place opens at its first chapter with
  something in it.
- **Footnotes hidden, offsets kept.** The app lays out chapters without the footnotes they link
  to (they open in a popup). The other blocks keep their offsets, so a Location means the same
  text with or without them.
- **Forced page breaks.** CSS `page-break-before` or `break-before` (`always`, `page`, `left`, `right`…) on a block starts a new page, unless the page is still empty.

</details>
